<h4> <b>3/12/2026</b> </h4>
<p>
    I went to some great talks today. Oh my oh my.
</p>
<p>
    "Best Practices for Character Navigation: Architecting for AAA Performance and Robustness" by Ben Sunshine-Hill from Havok was
    just a real broad crash course on character navigation AI. Navmesh construction, updating, solutions and their tradeoffs,
    A* and its alternatives, collision avoidance and its algorithms, mesh link 'traversals', BOIDs and other group navigation,
    and a bit on pathfinding for flying characters. He actually had three categories for that, which were characters in "Space" i.e.
    no reference to the ground. In which case it's pretty easy. Depending on the openness of the space, you may not need much path searching
    at all. The simplest BOID could work. Then "Sky" i.e. something flying relative to the ground like some flying enemy that's attacking a grounded
    player. Treating one of those as just a grounded unit, and then putting them up in the sky instead apparently works just fine, which I think is
    intuitive. And then "Cave", which are flying situations where you have some kind of labyrinthine tight 3D space to navigate. In which case he
    said that you'd need to use an octree to break up the space, but to be careful with its resolution. The players won't see it, so as long as
    it allows your AI to find paths, it doesn't need to be overly defined, as that can really eat up performance.
</p>
<p>
    He also talked about string pulling and path smoothing on A* searched paths. RVO and ORCA for collision avoidance. JPS+, subgoal grpahs, and HPA* on
    grid based pathing structures. Lots of things that I need to dig into on my own time. But one of the biggest, and kind of the most obvious in
    hindsight, things that he pointed out is that depending on your situation, characters don't need to path immediately. You can parallelize path
    solving asynchronously over multiple frames. If that results in your character taking some noticeable time to think, well... that's just how
    people act normally. When characters are trying to decide where to go next, it isn't weird for them to take a moment to think. That could have
    really saved me some grief on my second ETC project I think, where I had hundreds of pathfinding "gleebles" that were really hurting performance.
    I managed to get it there in the end, but budgeting out their pathing times as needed during moments of high load probably could have helped.
    He also had a funny point about cars and other vehicles. He talked about using rails for cars on streets, but then he mentioned "Dubins paths" for
    offroading vehicles, which represent the optimal trajectory from one transform to another for wheeled vehicles with a turning radius. However, they
    don't take into account collisions, so you have to use them in conjunction with something like A* or whatever. Or backing up when stuck a little, then
    repeathing. But he pointed out that this can cause a vehicle AI to get stuck in an alley or something, with its only option being to go back and forth and
    wiggle in the alley forever. The solution he proposed to that was just to... give up. If a car gets stuck in an alley, tries to path out three times and fails,
    and then just stops driving, that would just look like a human panicking and giving up just like they would in real life. Which I thought was amusing.
</p>
<p>
    The second talk I went to was a real doozy. I'm a big fan of <em>Limbus Company</em> so this one was great: "To Each Their Own Hell: The Journey of
    Writing Complex Characters in 'Limbus Company' (Presented by Project Moon)", by Eunkyong Han, the Lead Scenario Writer at Project Moon. She talked about
    her approach to writing and developing the wholeness of the characters in <em>Limbus Company</em>. And it felt like I was listening to an in-universe
    presentation on how to manifest E.G.O. For starters, the title of the talk wasn't just some poetic flair. She began by asking the question "What is Hell"?
    She went on to define "Hell" as personal to each individual. That characters have Virtues and Flaws. But Virtues can become Flaws and Flaws can become
    virtues in what she called a "Contradiction". The tension between virtues and flaws and the contradictions that make up a person give rise to their Hell.
    She used a bunch of examples from <em>Limbus Company</em>, so I did get spoiled on some of the parts I haven't reached yet. But one example she gave
    was Hong Lu's 'niceness'. Initially a Virtue, his 'niceness' is revealed to arise from his own detachment from his life, a consequence of his trauma. So
    his Virtue really acts as a Flaw. A Contradiction. If you've played <em>Limbus Company</em> then you know that exploring these Contradictions is really
    what the game's story is focused on. Eunkyong's approach to writing characters was to identify the Contradictions that made a character's Hell, and then
    find some kind of resolution. But she emphasized that a character 'resolving' their Hell shouldn't mean just overcoming it and then moving on as if they
    never had it. Rather, they should come to an understanding of their Hell, and use that understanding to guide their path forward.
</p>
<p>
    One example she went into depth on was Ishmael's Canto. She described that the writing process at Project Moon for her is that the director, Kim Ji Hoon,
    would describe to her what the big ideas and themes of the upcoming Canto were, what it needed to do and all the requirements, and then she would start
    drafting the specific scenarios that made it real. For Ishmael's Canto, she was given the requirement from the director that Ishmael could not end the
    Canto by murdering Captain Ahab. And so she said she had to completely scrap her first draft of the Canto, to the amusement of the audience. Apparently,
    that first draft had written itself into a place where it could not justify Ishmael not murdering Ahab. And when she examined why that was, she found that
    it was because it didn't really address Ishmael's Hell. She identified two questions that the story would have to address/resolve in order to actually achieve
    this. I don't remember exactly what they were, but they were something like "where does Ishmael's obsession come from?" and "what can she do to move past it?"
    (I think they were more complex than that, but you get the gist). Apparently she took some time and wrote in a journal from the perspective of Ishmael, going
    through her backstory of joining the whaling crew, falling under Captain Ahab's influence, and then losing everything. And by the end, she had identified that
    Ishmael's obsession with killing Ahab was really just an extension of Ahab's own obsession, and that despite having left the crew as its 'sole survivor' she was
    still trapped under the Captain's thumb. This Flaw of hers, her obsession, stemmed from this backstory, and in order to resolve her Hell, she would have to
    come to understand its source and allow it to move on from Ahab's influence, even if that meant sparing her life. And of course, if you've played the Canto,
    then you know that's basically what ends up happening. And Ishmael learned to "chart her own course".
</p>
<p>
    It was truly a fascinating talk. And you could really see in her philosophy where a lot of the 'Project-Moonness' of <em>Limbus Company</em> was coming from.
    She concluded her talk by making the point about not just overcoming and then throwing away a character's Hell being a core philosophy of Project Moon'S
    storytelling, the big thing that they wanted to impart to players. Then she talked about her own personal Flaws and her own Hell, which was her anxiety
    and constant dissatisfaction with her own writing. She reflected on how understanding that struggle in herself as a writer was something that she did in
    order to try and find her voice in her craft. It didn't sound like she had fully 'resolved' her own Hell. But even in The City, doing so and manifesting
    E.G.O. is pretty rare. I mean, mostly we only see Color Fixers do that, so it's not as if Project Moon (or Eunkyong) is saying that it's easy.
</p>
<p>
    So, basically, we all have Flaws and Virtues, those Flaws and Virtues can act in paradoxical ways creating Contradictions, the Contradictions of our
    characters make us fully-rounded people, but the tension they bring create our personal Hells, which we should all strive to understand in order to
    grow as people. And so, if you want your characters to have depth, you can think of them in this manner.
</p>
<p>
    Then I met up with someone who I had met for coffee a couple months ago while I was in Washington over Christmas. An ETC alum that my advisor connected me with.
    She's great. I attended the talk "Flipping the Script: Overhauling Sucker Punch's Writing Pipeline for 'Ghost of Yōtei'", where Roland Munsil talked about the
    narrative writing tool for games that he and some other devs made for Sucker Punch in order to fix a lot of the difficulties of merging narrative in <em>Ghost of Tsushima</em>.
    Apparently the way that Sucker Punch previously did writing was they had writers putting their scripts in Google Drive, designers marking them up with metadata in
    some Sony internal database service called "Line Up" or "Link Up" or something? And then eventually that was pushed to Perforce by the programmers. So... a real nightmare,
    with three 'sources of truth'. And the writers were very isolated from the process. The tool they made was just this really nifty program that allowed writers and designers
    to write in a slightly more technical screenwriting format (with hotkeys and all that similar to Final Draft), putting their scene direction comments and all that in. It would
    convert that into the data format that the game needed in order to run. The programmers, designers, and writers could now all look at the same source, see all their comments,
    understand more holistically what was going on with the script, and make changes to the one unified source. There were some nifty features he added, like a little pink diamond
    that indicated if a script was referenced anywhere, meaning it just had a trigger reference in the data somewhere. This was helpful for the designers, who could look down a list of
    conversation IDs and see which ones weren't linked yet and had to be, but it was also useful for writers who could see if a line was used yet. If it had no pink diamond, then
    it was guaranteed that no part of the game used it, so if it needed to be cut for writing reasons, the writer could do so without breaking any part of the game. It overall seemed
    like a great success. He had some great testimony from his writers, which is what realy matters. It's always interesting to here about how a tool can really bring a project team
    into such harmonious alignment.
</p>

<p>    
    The first one was about techinques for balancing trading card games (like Magic: The Gathering). The main idea was that 'algorithmic sorting',
    i.e. formulating some kind of mathematical expression that can calculate the relative powers of cards and allow you to create perfect
    balance is basically impossible. Assigning weighted values to various elements of a card (for example, MtG's power and toughness stats) can
    help you set up a basic power curve, but no TCG's mechanics are simple enough that all of their complexity can be objectively captured in this way.
    I mean, maybe someone will invent one, but that would be a strange beast indeed.
</p>
<p>
    The idea that the presenter (Nathan Heiss) proposed was something that he claimed that many experienced TCG designers did implicitly, and he was just
    formalizing the principle. He called the approach "Power Sorting". It sounded pretty logical to me, and I was a fan of the idea. Essentially,
    what you do is you first organize your cards into categories of related types. So in MtG, the example Heiss used was sorting 'counterspells' into category.
    Within that group, identify benchmark cards for each resource tier. So for the counterspell group in MtG, that would be like 'Dispel' at one mana and
    'Cryptic Command' at four mana. Now, there's some subjective evaluation that has to be done. Cryptic Command is certainly better than Dispel, because it
    is more flexible and can do more. So it makes sense that it costs more. The question of <em>how</em> much more seemed kind of vibes based. Like, Heiss already
    had two other counterspell cards to fill the two mana and three mana benchmarks. Cryptic Command was better than those two cards as well, so four mana was an
    appropriate place for it. And that leads in to the general idea of Power Sorting, which is just that having these benchmark cards established allows you as a 
    designer to better understand where a new card should fall cost-wise. A counterspell that is much worse than Cryptic Command shouldn't cost more than Cryptic Command,
    it's as simple as that. "Better" and "worse" are subjective for these kinds of things, as in there's no way to strictly quantify it, but you have to make that
    evaluation at some point, and this gives you a way to pin a number to it. And the nature of sorting cards this way means it becomes easier to balance
    new cards when you have a lot of preexisting cards, since those preexisting cards offer a wider range of benchmarks to measure against. The most 'shot in the dark'
    part of this framework is when you're creating a card that is more powerful than any other card has ever been. How much more mana should it cost than the current most
    expensive? That's really up to personal judgement and playtesting. But once you have that set, you now have another new benchmark that future cards can measure against.
</p>
<p>
    So that was the general idea. Heiss also talked about powercreep. It's hard to keep benchmarks sound forever. The strategies he proposed for dealing with
    powercreep once it got out of hand were classic. Things like ban cards, create new formats, just ignore it, print specific counters (silver bullets).
</p>
<p>
    The second talk was also very interesting. Alexey Drobyshevsky from Owlcat Games was talking about a system that he built to enable branching narratives with
    many sidequests and thousands of game states for Owlcat to build their RPGs with. The framework was built on a state unit that they called "Etudes".
    Essentially, Etudes are node blocks that are organized into a parent-child hierarchy. If an Etude's parent is not enabled, then that Etude is also not
    enabled. Etudes are also responsible for changing the gameworld in the way that the state they represent indicates the world should be changed. Etudes
    can also have conflicts that are resolved via priority values.
</p>
<p>
    The example scenario he gave to demonstrate Etudes was this: there's a character, the Rogue, who is in the Tavern. When the player enters the Tavern,
    the Rogue should be there. The Rogue gives the player a quest, and when that quest is completed it can resolve in a way that means the Rogue should
    stay in the Tavern and in a way that means the Rogue should no longer be in the Tavern. In addition, there is a world event that can occur where a brawl
    breaks out in the Tavern, in which case the Rogue should not be present no matter what.
</p>
<p>
    The way the Etudes were set up, there was a parent Etude that was the parent of all the Rogue states. It shared a conflict called 'tavern_floor' with
    and Etude that represented the Tavern Brawl state. It had a much lower priority than the Tavern Brawl Etude. Therefore, if the Tavern Brawl Etude was ever
    active, then the parent Rogue Etude would be disabled, and all logic related to the Rogue would never be enabled. The Rogue parent Etude had a Rogue quest
    Etude that spawned the Rogue, and that had two children that represented the two possible states. The quest end state that meant the Rogue should despawn would
    despawn the Rogue when it was completed. There was a small system for deferring the "completion" of an Etude that was otherwise ready to resolve, in this case
    that specific quest completion Etude would only resolve once the player left the Tavern, so that the Rogue didn't just blip out of existence in front of them.
</p>
<p>
    I really liked the structure of this system. It takes a mess of global flags and structures them into a hierarchy that implicitly captures mutual exclusions
    and implications. They also did some things to make it so that when certain states were active you could trace the chain of reasons why easier. I wasn't super
    clear on the exact way that quests and the linking of quests was implemented, but I think that if I were to try and implement a system like this for my own projects
    (like for <em>Onion Future</em> perhaps), I could figure out a way that I would want to do it.
</p>
<p>
    Overall, some very interesting stuff. I did also go to an AI Game Programmer roundtable (or rather, the room was already packed, but the line was very long, so
    a second impromptu roundtable started on the balcony). And to clarify, it was for classic enemy AI stuff (descision trees, state machines, nav mesh, etc.), not
    ML GenAI type stuff. But it did end up devolving into that... There is a whole different world out there. There's so many AI company ads in SF! It's wild.
    Truly, a different culture.
